Mobile item — interactions

What the item page does under a finger: every gesture, every control, and the rule behind each one. The design is docs/mobile-item.html and the order of work is docs/mobile-item-plan.html; this is the third of the three, and it records behaviour rather than appearance.

How to read this. Each entry is a behaviour, then the reason it is that way. The reasons matter more than the behaviours: most of them were bought with a review round on a real phone, and a future change that does not know the reason will buy the same round again. Every phase now has a section; the unbuilt ones carry their tag. The one exception is an entry tagged designed: behaviour that has been decided and handed to an issue but is not in the build yet. It loses the tag when it lands.

0 · True on every screen

Submitting a source is one control, and it is always within reach designed · DIA-436 · not built

הגישו מקור is the same outlined pill wherever it appears, and it opens the one submission sheet, which returns the reader by name to the page they left. On a reading slide the card decides where the pill is: a card whose reading fits shows it beside המקורות in a split button row; a card whose reading is cut keeps its single יותר מידע pill, and the control sits at the end of the reading sheet, after the sources. Slide 4 has its full pill; slide 6 has a quiet line above the sign-off; slide 5 has the newsletter instead.

Roy's rule: this is not slide 4's button but the deck's, so it must be one shape and never lose its home. The first draft put it only at the end of the sheet, because the reader who knows better is the one who read to the end of the sources — and Roy caught the hole: a reader whose card fits never opens the sheet, and for them the control had no home at all. The card already knows whether it fits, so it decides. The cut state adds no chrome for a rare action; the fit state has the room. Slide 6 gets the quiet form because a second pill under share would compete with the slide's one gold action.

The page itself never scrolls

The deck is a fixed frame at 100dvh. Every scroll on the item page belongs to a region inside a slide — the horizontal track, slide 2's body, and from Phase 5 the stage stacks. The document does not move.

100dvh and not 100vh: iOS collapses its URL bar as you scroll, and vh keeps the pre-collapse height, so the deck would sit taller than the window and the footer would hide under the bar.

Nothing is gesture-only

Every move the deck can make by gesture has a second route that a screen reader or a keyboard can take: six labelled sections in DOM order, footer labels that are real links, arrow keys on a hardware keyboard.

On a phone a screen-reader swipe means "next element", not "next slide". A deck whose only route between slides is a horizontal drag is a deck a screen-reader user cannot leave.

Reduced motion removes motion, never state

Under prefers-reduced-motion animated travel becomes an instant landing — jump mode's live scrub, the chip landing a card, the deck's own smooth scroll. Anything that shows something, such as jump mode's dim, stays.

The dim is not decoration; it says the deck is in a different mode. Removing it would remove the only sign that the mode is on.

The finger is not the only reader

Gestures are proved with real touch events, not mouse drags, and on measured geometry rather than screenshots.

A mouse does not pan a scroll container. A mouse-driven test passed against a build where the body had quietly become a horizontal scroller and the swipe did not work at all.

On a light phone the gate keeps its own ink designed · DIA-403 · not built

The deck follows the phone's colour scheme; the gate does not — it is dark in both. While the gate is in view the chrome wears the gate's light ink, whatever the theme, and as the cover leaves the ink travels to the theme's in step with the finger: p of the way across, p theme ink. It holds if the finger stops, runs backwards on the way back, and under reduced motion swaps at the midpoint with the labels. On a dark phone the two inks are the same and nothing is visible.

On a light-mode phone the chrome took the light theme's dark ink and sat on the gate's dark photograph: the dots, the swipe hint and the credit were gone, on the one screen whose job is to say "go on". Two fixes were mocked. The other — the deck dark on every phone — was ruled first, built, and reversed the same day in favour of this one. Its known cost is accepted: half way through the first swipe the chrome is a mid grey, weak over the photograph and over the paper alike, for the middle of one gesture. It also leaves the light deck as it is, which nobody has designed or reviewed. Ruled 21 September (Roy).

Phase 2 · The deck, the chrome, the gestures done

DIA-377. The frame, the chrome that rides on it, and every way between slides. Nothing here renders incident data, which is why it could be judged on its own.

Moving between slides

GestureWhat happens
Horizontal swipeThe main route. A horizontal scroll-snap track; the slide travels with the finger and lands on a snap point.
A fast flickMoves one slide, never two. scroll-snap-stop: always.
Footer ‹ ›Real links, labelled with the destination slide's name. Slide 1 has no previous label and slide 6 no next; those ends are bare rather than disabled.
Arrow keysMapped spatially, so in RTL the right arrow goes back and the left arrow forward.
Tapping a dotNothing. The dots are a counter and jump mode's strip, not six buttons — at six dots across 390px a tap target would be smaller than a fingertip.

A slide never lands off its snap point

Every programmatic move is scrollIntoView({inline:'start'}) on the slide element, and a settle() on scrollend corrects anything more than half a pixel out.

Three separate bugs came from calculating instead of measuring. i * clientWidth is not a slide's position — the frame is fluid between 320 and 599 so widths carry fractions, and a programmatic scroll is not re-snapped. scrollBy with a measured delta is worse: scroll-snap-stop: always caps it at one snap point, so a jump to slide 4 arrived at slide 2.

The chrome is the destination's from the moment the destination is chosen

While the deck is driving itself towards a slide, the scroll handler reports nothing. The landing is recorded once, on scrollend.

Without it, tapping the footer showed the destination, flashed the slide it came from as the animation crossed the midpoint, then settled. The reader reads that as a bug, because it is one.

The footer's labels crossfade under the finger designed · DIA-401 · not built

The labels of the two slides a swipe is between are both present, stacked in the same slot, and the track's position sets the mix: 30% of the way across, the outgoing labels are at 70% and the incoming at 30%. The two always sum to 1. Stop mid-swipe and the blend holds. The layer above half takes the taps. Reduced motion keeps today's swap at the midpoint. The dots are not part of it.

The labels used to be replaced in a single frame, mid-gesture. A fade-out followed by a fade-in was tried first and rejected on the phone: for a moment the slot is empty, and the eye catches it. A timed true crossfade was the second candidate; the finger-driven one won because it has no timer at all, the same reason the cover leaves whole. This amends the rule above for the footer's labels only — that rule exists to stop a flicker during driven scrolls, and a blend that follows position cannot flicker. It still governs the dots. Chosen 21 September (Roy).

Leaving the gate

The photograph leaves with the cover designed · DIA-385 · not built

The gate's ground belongs to slide 1 and travels with it — full frame, under the breadcrumb, the dots and the footer — as one object with one clean edge. No fade, no timer, nothing linked to scroll progress. Coming back to the gate is the same motion reversed. The no-photo ground behaves identically.

With it: a slide's clearance for the chrome is its own and does not change mid-swipe. Nothing visible moves vertically during a horizontal swipe.

It is the one transition every reader makes. As built, the ground sits behind the whole frame and the slides have none of their own, so slide 2's text crosses the photograph until the midpoint, where the photo fades on a 180ms timer and the gate's title drops about 10px because every slide shares one measured clearance. A dissolve tied to the finger was prototyped beside this and rejected: the text still crosses the image for the first third of the gesture. A cover that leaves whole never puts text over the photograph, and it is what a post's first image does — which is what this screen is. Chosen on a phone, 21 September (Roy).

The gate says which way to go designed · DIA-398 · not built

The gate's hint reads החליקו להמשך with a left-pointing chevron — the same chevron as the next-slide links on every other slide — in place of החליקו לצדדים. And if the reader has done nothing for 5 seconds, the track slides about 38px toward slide 2 and returns, once. Any touch before it fires cancels it for good; it never runs on a return to the gate, on a deep link that opened elsewhere, or under reduced motion. It moves the track only: no hash, no chrome, and the scroll handler does not read it as the reader moving.

The gate is the front door for a reader arriving cold from a post, and the old hint named both sides when only one works. The chevron points at where the next slide is, which is also how a Hebrew book turns, so the footer says one thing about direction on all six slides. The nudge is the deck's single use of a timer: "nothing runs on a timer" is about pacing content, and this paces nothing — it shows an edge to someone who has not moved. Both halves chosen on a phone, 21 September (Roy).

Jump mode — the dot row

Press and hold, scrub, release

A 400ms press on the dot row arms the mode. The page then scrubs live under the finger — the deck follows the dot the thumb is over — and release lands there. The mode ends with the finger: there is nothing to dismiss.

Armed, the strip goes to full strength and the deck behind it drops to 40% over 120ms. Arming also takes scroll-snap-type off the track.

Snap and scrub fight otherwise: the track keeps pulling to the nearest slide while the thumb is still moving. The dim is state, so it survives reduced motion; the live scrub does not, and becomes a jump on release.

A finger that travels before the hold is a swipe, not an arm

Movement during the 400ms cancels the press.

The dot row sits in the bottom quarter of the screen, which is where a thumb naturally swipes. Without the cancel, half the swipes started down there would arm instead.

The strip refuses the browser its own gestures

-webkit-touch-callout:none, user-select:none, touch-action:none on the dot row.

A long press on iOS otherwise raises the selection callout and the magnifier, on top of the mode the press just armed. There is no haptics API in Safari, so the feedback is visual or it does not exist.

The strip opens under the thumb, names the landing, and can be abandoned designed · DIA-399 · not built

On arming, the dots spread from a 13px pitch to about 30px, and the row shifts so the dot under the thumb does not move — the others open out around it. A pill floats above the strip, clear of the thumb, carrying the name of the slide a release would land on; no number, the dots are still the counter. Dragging about 70px above the strip abandons the jump: the deck returns to where it was armed, the label reads שחררו לביטול, and a release writes nothing. Coming back down resumes the scrub.

Six dots at 13px is 71px for the whole deck, under the thumb doing the scrubbing, and the only landing feedback was a ring on a dot the finger covered. A mode that always lands is also a mode a reader cannot back out of once they see where it is taking them. This is the dot row only; the stage rail is looked at with slide 3. Approved on a phone, 21 September (Roy).

The breadcrumb

The root is a link home; the parent waits for a page to exist designed · DIA-400 · not built

7 באוקטובר is a real link to the home page, with a 44px touch target that moves nothing. The parent crumb does nothing and must not look tappable-and-broken: no pressed state, not focusable. The leaf is never a link. The look does not change.

A breadcrumb that does not navigate is a broken affordance, and until slide 6 it was the deck's only possible way out sideways. The parent has nowhere to go: only home and the item page exist, and the outer layers are designed after the item on purpose, because each outer layer is there to wrap its children. When a parent page exists, the crumb links to it. Ruled 21 September (Roy).

The breadcrumb never loses its leaf designed · DIA-407 · not built

When the path does not fit its one line, the parent shortens with an ellipsis. The root, the chevrons and the leaf keep their full width, the gutters hold, and the chrome's height does not change. The full parent name stays available to a screen reader.

The line overflowed to the left, so what got cut was כשל מס׳ N — the one thing the breadcrumb is there to say. It never showed on the dev build because the test pool's parents are short; the live ledger's longest, at 37 characters, puts the leaf 21px off the screen at 390 wide and 51px off at 360. Approved 21 September (Roy).

The URL and the Back button

One entry on the history stack, however far the reader travels

Slides are hashes — #3 for a slide, #3-s2 for a stage. The first move pushes a history entry; every move after it replaces. Returning to the slide the reader arrived on is a history.back() rather than a write.

Most readers arrive from a post, inside an in-app browser. Two presses of Back have to get them out from anywhere in the deck. A push per slide would bury the exit six deep.

The gate is the bare URL, never #1

A cold deep link opens on its slide and survives a refresh.

One canonical address per item. #1 and no hash would be two links to the same screen, and both would be shared.

Phase 3 · Slide 1 — השער done

DIA-378. The first screen carrying a real incident, and deliberately the least interactive one in the deck.

Nothing on the gate is a control

The stage rail is a picture of where the item stands, not a way to go anywhere. The photo credit is text. The only interactive things on the screen are the deck's own chrome.

It is the screen a reader lands on from a post, and the one place where the item has to say what it is before it asks for anything. Its whole job is to be read. Making the rail's labels ways into their stages was proposed and mocked on 21 September and refused: the reader is taken through the slides in the order they were written, not handed a way to jump ahead of the overview (Roy).

A long credit takes two rows inside the footer it already has designed · DIA-404 · not built

A credit that does not fit on one row takes two — 9.5px on an 11px line, left-aligned, capped at about 225px so the hint keeps its room — and both rows fit the footer slot's existing 22px. The footer's height, its rule and the dots do not move. Never an ellipsis, never a third row; a credit that fits on one row is unchanged.

A PikiWiki credit runs to about 75 characters, ran off the left edge and under the hint, and a licence credit cannot be truncated. Letting the footer grow was mocked first and refused: a footer taller on the gate than on slide 2 makes the chrome jump on the first swipe, which is worse than small type. Quick and dirty, on purpose (Roy, 21 September).

Edge-anchored things are anchored physically

The rail sits against the right edge by a physical value and is asserted on a measured getBoundingClientRect().

§2's RTL trap: padding-inline-end and align-items:flex-end both resolve left in RTL. It has cost two review rounds, and a logical property that reads correctly in the source is exactly the kind of thing nobody re-reads.

Phase 4 · Slide 2 — סקירת הכשל done

DIA-381. The first body slide, and the first screen with a scroller of its own — which is where most of the interaction rules below come from. Slides 3 and 4 inherit them.

The chip is docked; everything under it travels

The slide title stays where it is. The body and the sources carousel scroll together as one column.

The carousel is the end of the reading, not a shelf above it. Docked to the frame it read as something the text ran under. On an item that overruns the column it now waits below the last sentence; on one that fits it still falls to the foot of the column, which is how §5's screen draws it.

The axis is the engine's, and it is wrong — DIA-383. A scroll a few degrees off vertical is read as a swipe most of the time, and a thumb travelling right goes forward on the gate but backward on slide 2, because a nested scroller changes the handoff. Both are open.

An axis lock was written and reverted. It declared touch-action: pan-y on the scroller — the only lever that reliably takes the axis away from the engine — and carried the sideways drag in code, with sideways having to beat down by 1.6:1. It measured correctly and felt wrong in the hand: pan-y is all-or-nothing, so taking the axis means owning the swipe's inertia too, and a hand-driven pan does not have the engine's momentum curve. The issue records what the attempt is still worth, so the next one does not start here.

The body scrolls down and never sideways

The slide gives up its own side padding and the three parts take it back, so the scroller's content is exactly as wide as the scroller.

overflow-y:auto makes the browser compute overflow-x to auto as well, and the carousel's bleed gave that axis 40px to travel. The browser hands a horizontal drag to the nearest scroller that can take it, so the deck never saw the swipe and slides could only be changed from the bezels. touch-action: pan-y alone would not have fixed it — it forbids horizontal panning for the whole ancestor chain, so it would have killed the swipe rather than passing it on.

A source chip lands its card; it does not leave the deck

Tapping a chip scrolls the carousel to that claim's card and marks it. The card itself carries the outbound link, one tap further on. A chip resting on several claims lands the first of them, and the rest are adjacent, because the carousel is deduplicated in order of first appearance.

Most readers arrive inside an in-app browser, where leaving is close to one-way. This is also what makes the carousel load-bearing, and why it is never shed. The rail is moved by a measured delta rather than arithmetic: it is RTL, and scrollLeft's sign there is not worth remembering.

The carousel keeps its own horizontal axis

A drag started on the carousel scrolls the carousel, not the deck, and does not chain to the deck at either end.

It is a carousel; owning sideways drags on itself is the whole point. Chaining at the end would fling the reader to the next slide while they were browsing sources.

The reading model — replaces the scrolling body

Ruled 21 September, after four mock rounds. Roy's direction for slides 2 and 3: take what Google Search's AI Overview does well — clean, readable, focused — and fix the vertical scroll. The look (two sizes, one ink, glyph citations, the card and the sheet as drawn objects) goes through the UI designer and the spec first, as DIA-412; what follows is the behaviour. When it lands, the entries above about the scrolling body, the sources riding with the text and the in-flow drawer's place on the slide are superseded, and DIA-383 closes.

A slide never scrolls designed · DIA-413 · not built

A slide's content is a card that fits the frame. What overruns is cut with a fade and a button — יותר מידע with the source count; content that fits shows no fade and the button reads המקורות. The full text, and the sources carousel after it, open in a reading sheet above the deck: one plain vertical scroller, no snap, no horizontal scroller around it. The × and Back close it; Back closes the sheet and nothing else, and the deck's one-entry history rule is whole again afterwards.

Reading inside the slide put a vertical scroller inside a horizontal track, and every fault of these slides came from that: the casual scroll read as a swipe (DIA-383), the snap fighting the reader at a stage boundary, the stack naming the wrong stage mid-read. An axis lock was tried and reverted because owning the axis means owning its inertia. This removes the crossing instead of refereeing it — a sheet above the track has no horizontal ancestor, so there is nothing for a diagonal thumb to trigger. It is also what the AI Overview does on a phone: a lead, a fade, "more", and the rest in a full-screen view with a ×.

The sheet opens where the slide was, and says so when it must designed · DIA-413 · not built

The sheet takes the card's geometry — its bar ends where the card's content begins, its column is the card's column — and opens unscrolled, so every line is exactly where it was on the slide. Two entrances. From the button, a deliberate act, it appears in place: a short fade, nothing moves. From a tap on a passage it is a cover from the bottom up — an opaque panel with a shadowed leading edge, the slide dimming behind it — landing with the text where it was. It leaves the way it came. Reduced motion makes both instant.

Alignment was Roy's correction to a first version that scrolled the sheet to the tapped passage: keeping the text still "helps with orientation much more than scrolling". Aligned and faded, the change of mode became invisible — "too smooth", a reader might not realise they had entered a different mode until they could not swipe sideways. The cover is the signal, kept for the entrance that was not announced by a button. A panel with an edge and a shadow reads as a sheet rising; identical text sliding over itself without one reads as a glitch, which is why the first slide-up was dropped.

The whole cited passage is the target designed · DIA-414 · not built

The citation is a small glyph; the tap target is the whole passage it ends — the span the annotation already wraps. The passage tints while pressed and stays tinted while its drawer is open. On the slide, a tap opens the sheet at that passage with its drawer open, once the cover has landed; if the drawer would fall below the fold, the sheet lands aligned first and only then makes the minimum scroll. In the sheet, a tap toggles the drawer. A long-press still selects text. For a screen reader the glyph is the one control.

A 20px glyph is the right size to look at and the wrong size to hit (Roy: "apply the hit slop to the entire sentence"). Taps on running text were otherwise unused, and the reader who taps a sentence is asking what it rests on — which the drawer answers and a jump to the sources at the foot of the sheet did not.

The drawer is an excerpt, and the quote is a clipping designed · DIA-426 · not built

A passage's sources open beneath it with no box: a thin rule in the source-type colour, a line naming the outlet, its type and the date, the quote set as a strip of newsprint — paper ground, black italic serif, a torn edge top and bottom — and לפתיחת המקור as the one link. Several sources stack as several excerpts. Everything else about the drawer (one at a time, the passage as the target, the minimum scroll, closing with the sheet) stands.

Four directions were tried on a phone — the excerpt, the carousel's card, a tray at the foot that leaves the text still, and a popover on the glyph. Roy chose the excerpt: the drawer stays in the reading flow and carries no chrome of its own, so the quote is what the eye lands on. The clipping is the desktop's paper brought to the phone, concentrated on the quote alone: it says "this is someone else's words, from somewhere else" without a label saying so. A gradient fade was tried at the edges and replaced by a tear, and a regular zigzag by the irregular one; the ink went from 40% grey to black because the quote is evidence, not decoration.

The sheet opens under a moving finger, and leaves three ways designed · DIA-427 · not built

On slides 2 and 4 a swipe up opens the sheet forty pixels in, while the finger is still down, and that same finger keeps scrolling the sheet — the reading starts in the motion that asked for it. Three ways out, each under the finger with a spring-back short of its threshold: swipe up once the sheet is at its end and it moves up and fades until it dissolves; swipe sideways anywhere and it does the same to the side; swipe down at the top and it slides back down the way it came, unfaded. Slide 3 keeps the button as its only way in.

The button is a deliberate act; a swipe up is what a thumb does to see more, and the sheet is more. Opening early and handing the scroll to the same finger is what makes it one motion instead of two (Roy: "before the finger leaves the screen"). The end-of-sheet swipe closes a loop — a reader who reached the end and keeps going is done. Sideways exists because a sheet above a horizontal track is the one place a horizontal swipe does nothing, and doing nothing there reads as broken. Slide 3 is excluded because its vertical axis already pages stages, and a rule like "only on the last page" is invisible. Pull-down was DIA-417, Post-MVP for lack of a thumb; it now has one and ships with the rest.

Phase 5 · Slide 3 — מה נעשה מאז in progress

DIA-384. The stages the item reached, one page each. The first screen with two scroll axes at once, and the pattern slide 4 inherits in Phase 6.

One gesture, two jobs, and no code between them

The stack is a single scroller with nested scroll-snap: each stage page is a snap point, scroll-snap-stop: always. Scrolling on at the bottom of a stage takes the reader to the next; scrolling back at the top takes them to the previous. There is no listener, no threshold and no boundary detection anywhere in the slide.

Deliberate, and the one architectural instruction this phase carried. DIA-383 is open on the deck's own axis decision; a second gesture layer here would mean fixing the same thing twice and in two places. The boundary is a property of the scroller, so whatever DIA-383 eventually does, this slide inherits it.

A stage reopens where it was left

A stage the reader has not been to opens at its top. One they have already read reopens where they left it, for as long as the page is open. A deep link to #3-s2 is a first visit. A landing through jump mode is an ordinary entry and obeys the memory. A stage shorter than the frame has nothing to remember. Nothing survives a reload.

It is what makes scrolling back up bearable: the reader returns to the foot of the stage they just read rather than above everything in it. The position is recorded for whichever page is at the top, every frame the reader moves — and never while the stack is scrolling itself, because a driven scroll passes through positions nobody chose.

The locator is an indicator, not a control

Tapping a rung does nothing. Five slots; only the stages belonging to this slide are drawn and the rest keep their place as invisible spacers. One ring, on the stage being viewed.

Held slots are why a rung never moves between slide 3 and slide 4 — the same stage is in the same place on both, so the ladder reads as one object across a swipe. The current stage lost its own ring on 20 September: splitting reached from unreached made it redundant, since here it is always the last rung drawn.

The pill's row keeps its height whether or not the pill is in it

חזרה לשלב הנוכחי appears only when the reader is off the current stage, with its arrow pointing the way. The row it sits in is always there.

DIA-379 again: a chrome that changes height resizes the scroller, and resizing a scroll container mid-scroll makes the engine recompute its snap positions. That was an overshoot on the deck; here it would be a stage page jumping under a thumb.

The footer's arrows are the route for a reader who does not scroll

↑↓ שלבים in the middle of the docked footer. Two real buttons, drawn as the screens draw the glyphs.

Nothing on the deck may be gesture-only. The footer is rendered once as docked chrome rather than per slide, so the control is given to the deck and talks to the stack through an event — which is also why slide 4 gets it in Phase 6 without a second implementation.

The URL's tail follows the page

Moving between stages rewrites the hash to #3-s2 and so on, under the same push-once rule as the slides. A refresh lands back on the stage that was showing.

The deck owns the URL and the stack owns which page it is showing. The stack publishes its page on the deck's own root and the deck reads it from there, so neither has to import the other to agree.

Every stage is one frame, and the stack pages designed · DIA-416 · not built

With every stage page a card one frame tall, the stack is uniform: mandatory snap, one stage per gesture, never at rest between two. The locator, the hash's tail, the arrows and the back-to-current pill behave as before and stop being wrong. The per-stage scroll memory is retired — a page has no inside to remember. Slide 4 inherits all of it.

Measured on the build: a 500px stack holding a 1261px page. Near its end the engine took the scroll and snapped to the next stage, and snapped away again on the way back; and because the current page was "the one whose top is nearest", 700px into that page the ring, the URL and the pill all named the next stage. "A stage reopens where it was left" existed to make that bearable. Two paged axes still cross here, but what made DIA-383 bite was reading scrolls — long and sloppy — not paging flicks; if a diagonal flick still misfires on a phone, that is a new and smaller issue.

In the sheet, the ladder is the way between stages designed · DIA-430 · not built

While a stage's reading sheet is open, tapping a rung of the ladder swaps the sheet to that stage without leaving it: the drawer closes, the ring moves, the reading fades out with a small nudge in the direction of the jump and the next stage fades in at its top, the chip in the bar changes, and the stack underneath moves to that stage without a sound — so closing the sheet lands where the reading ended. It is the only way between stages in the sheet; there is no vertical paging there. On the slide the ladder stays an indicator. Only reached rungs exist in the sheet, and each one's target is the whole rung pitch, reaching into the column.

Roy's ruling: a reader in the sheet should be able to move through the stages "without ever breaking from it". The sheet is a plain scroller with no vertical paging, so the ladder — already on screen, already naming the stages by position — is the one thing that can carry that without a new control. The stack follows because "never breaking" would break at the exit otherwise: a reader who jumped two stages up and closed the sheet would land two stages back. The nudge exists so a jump reads as a direction and not a reload; if it proves fussy under a thumb, a plain crossfade is the fallback.

Phase 6 · Slide 4 — מה עוד לא נעשה done

An unreached stage is one frame, and says what the stage is designed · DIA-425 · not built

The unreached page is a card exactly one frame tall, like every stage page after DIA-416, and it never scrolls. Under the lead sits a dim, dashed box at the vertical centre of the screen — clamped so it never touches the lead above or the age below — holding two short paragraphs: לא מצאנו תיעוד לכך ש… completed by the stage's definition, then why that matters, opening עד אז. The age of the last reached stage sits at the foot of the card, centred: the hourglass, the number, the label under the number. If the card would overflow, the second paragraph goes first.
