Mobile item — interactions

What the item page does under a finger: every gesture, every control, and the rule behind each one. The design is docs/mobile-item.html and the order of work is docs/mobile-item-plan.html; this is the third of the three, and it records behaviour rather than appearance.

How to read this. Each entry is a behaviour, then the reason it is that way. The reasons matter more than the behaviours: most of them were bought with a review round on a real phone, and a future change that does not know the reason will buy the same round again. Phases 5 to 8 are absent on purpose — nothing is written here until it exists. 0 · True on every screen

The page itself never scrolls

The deck is a fixed frame at 100dvh. Every scroll on the item page belongs to a region inside a slide — the horizontal track, slide 2's body, and from Phase 5 the stage stacks. The document does not move.

100dvh and not 100vh: iOS collapses its URL bar as you scroll, and vh keeps the pre-collapse height, so the deck would sit taller than the window and the footer would hide under the bar.

Nothing is gesture-only

Every move the deck can make by gesture has a second route that a screen reader or a keyboard can take: six labelled sections in DOM order, footer labels that are real links, arrow keys on a hardware keyboard.

On a phone a screen-reader swipe means "next element", not "next slide". A deck whose only route between slides is a horizontal drag is a deck a screen-reader user cannot leave.

Reduced motion removes motion, never state

Under prefers-reduced-motion animated travel becomes an instant landing — jump mode's live scrub, the chip landing a card, the deck's own smooth scroll. Anything that shows something, such as jump mode's dim, stays.

The dim is not decoration; it says the deck is in a different mode. Removing it would remove the only sign that the mode is on.

The finger is not the only reader

Gestures are proved with real touch events, not mouse drags, and on measured geometry rather than screenshots.

A mouse does not pan a scroll container. A mouse-driven test passed against a build where the body had quietly become a horizontal scroller and the swipe did not work at all.

Phase 2 · The deck, the chrome, the gestures done

DIA-377. The frame, the chrome that rides on it, and every way between slides. Nothing here renders incident data, which is why it could be judged on its own.

Moving between slides

GestureWhat happens
Horizontal swipeThe main route. A horizontal scroll-snap track; the slide travels with the finger and lands on a snap point.
A fast flickMoves one slide, never two. scroll-snap-stop: always.
Footer ‹ ›Real links, labelled with the destination slide's name. Slide 1 has no previous label and slide 6 no next; those ends are bare rather than disabled.
Arrow keysMapped spatially, so in RTL the right arrow goes back and the left arrow forward.
Tapping a dotNothing. The dots are a counter and jump mode's strip, not six buttons — at six dots across 390px a tap target would be smaller than a fingertip.

A slide never lands off its snap point

Every programmatic move is scrollIntoView({inline:'start'}) on the slide element, and a settle() on scrollend corrects anything more than half a pixel out.

Three separate bugs came from calculating instead of measuring. i * clientWidth is not a slide's position — the frame is fluid between 320 and 599 so widths carry fractions, and a programmatic scroll is not re-snapped. scrollBy with a measured delta is worse: scroll-snap-stop: always caps it at one snap point, so a jump to slide 4 arrived at slide 2.

The chrome is the destination's from the moment the destination is chosen

While the deck is driving itself towards a slide, the scroll handler reports nothing. The landing is recorded once, on scrollend.

Without it, tapping the footer showed the destination, flashed the slide it came from as the animation crossed the midpoint, then settled. The reader reads that as a bug, because it is one.

Leaving the gate

The photograph leaves with the cover designed · DIA-385 · not built

The gate's ground belongs to slide 1 and travels with it — full frame, under the breadcrumb, the dots and the footer — as one object with one clean edge. No fade, no timer, nothing linked to scroll progress. Coming back to the gate is the same motion reversed. The no-photo ground behaves identically.

With it: a slide's clearance for the chrome is its own and does not change mid-swipe. Nothing visible moves vertically during a horizontal swipe.

It is the one transition every reader makes. As built, the ground sits behind the whole frame and the slides have none of their own, so slide 2's text crosses the photograph until the midpoint, where the photo fades on a 180ms timer and the gate's title drops about 10px because every slide shares one measured clearance. A dissolve tied to the finger was prototyped beside this and rejected: the text still crosses the image for the first third of the gesture. A cover that leaves whole never puts text over the photograph, and it is what a post's first image does — which is what this screen is. Chosen on a phone, 21 September (Roy).

Jump mode — the dot row

Press and hold, scrub, release

A 400ms press on the dot row arms the mode. The page then scrubs live under the finger — the deck follows the dot the thumb is over — and release lands there. The mode ends with the finger: there is nothing to dismiss.

Armed, the strip goes to full strength and the deck behind it drops to 40% over 120ms. Arming also takes scroll-snap-type off the track.

Snap and scrub fight otherwise: the track keeps pulling to the nearest slide while the thumb is still moving. The dim is state, so it survives reduced motion; the live scrub does not, and becomes a jump on release.

A finger that travels before the hold is a swipe, not an arm

Movement during the 400ms cancels the press.

The dot row sits in the bottom quarter of the screen, which is where a thumb naturally swipes. Without the cancel, half the swipes started down there would arm instead.

The strip refuses the browser its own gestures

-webkit-touch-callout:none, user-select:none, touch-action:none on the dot row.

A long press on iOS otherwise raises the selection callout and the magnifier, on top of the mode the press just armed. There is no haptics API in Safari, so the feedback is visual or it does not exist.

The URL and the Back button

One entry on the history stack, however far the reader travels

Slides are hashes — #3 for a slide, #3-s2 for a stage. The first move pushes a history entry; every move after it replaces. Returning to the slide the reader arrived on is a history.back() rather than a write.

Most readers arrive from a post, inside an in-app browser. Two presses of Back have to get them out from anywhere in the deck. A push per slide would bury the exit six deep.

The gate is the bare URL, never #1

A cold deep link opens on its slide and survives a refresh.

One canonical address per item. #1 and no hash would be two links to the same screen, and both would be shared.

Phase 3 · Slide 1 — השער done

DIA-378. The first screen carrying a real incident, and deliberately the least interactive one in the deck.

Nothing on the gate is a control

The stage rail is a picture of where the item stands, not a way to go anywhere. The photo credit is text. The only interactive things on the screen are the deck's own chrome.

It is the screen a reader lands on from a post, and the one place where the item has to say what it is before it asks for anything. Its whole job is to be read.

Edge-anchored things are anchored physically

The rail sits against the right edge by a physical value and is asserted on a measured getBoundingClientRect().

§2's RTL trap: padding-inline-end and align-items:flex-end both resolve left in RTL. It has cost two review rounds, and a logical property that reads correctly in the source is exactly the kind of thing nobody re-reads.

Phase 4 · Slide 2 — סקירת הכשל done

DIA-381. The first body slide, and the first screen with a scroller of its own — which is where most of the interaction rules below come from. Slides 3 and 4 inherit them.

The chip is docked; everything under it travels

The slide title stays where it is. The body and the sources carousel scroll together as one column.

The carousel is the end of the reading, not a shelf above it. Docked to the frame it read as something the text ran under. On an item that overruns the column it now waits below the last sentence; on one that fits it still falls to the foot of the column, which is how §5's screen draws it.

The axis is the engine's, and it is wrong — DIA-383. A scroll a few degrees off vertical is read as a swipe most of the time, and a thumb travelling right goes forward on the gate but backward on slide 2, because a nested scroller changes the handoff. Both are open.

An axis lock was written and reverted. It declared touch-action: pan-y on the scroller — the only lever that reliably takes the axis away from the engine — and carried the sideways drag in code, with sideways having to beat down by 1.6:1. It measured correctly and felt wrong in the hand: pan-y is all-or-nothing, so taking the axis means owning the swipe's inertia too, and a hand-driven pan does not have the engine's momentum curve. The issue records what the attempt is still worth, so the next one does not start here.

The body scrolls down and never sideways

The slide gives up its own side padding and the three parts take it back, so the scroller's content is exactly as wide as the scroller.

overflow-y:auto makes the browser compute overflow-x to auto as well, and the carousel's bleed gave that axis 40px to travel. The browser hands a horizontal drag to the nearest scroller that can take it, so the deck never saw the swipe and slides could only be changed from the bezels. touch-action: pan-y alone would not have fixed it — it forbids horizontal panning for the whole ancestor chain, so it would have killed the swipe rather than passing it on.

A source chip lands its card; it does not leave the deck

Tapping a chip scrolls the carousel to that claim's card and marks it. The card itself carries the outbound link, one tap further on. A chip resting on several claims lands the first of them, and the rest are adjacent, because the carousel is deduplicated in order of first appearance.

Most readers arrive inside an in-app browser, where leaving is close to one-way. This is also what makes the carousel load-bearing, and why it is never shed. The rail is moved by a measured delta rather than arithmetic: it is RTL, and scrollLeft's sign there is not worth remembering.

The carousel keeps its own horizontal axis

A drag started on the carousel scrolls the carousel, not the deck, and does not chain to the deck at either end.

It is a carousel; owning sideways drags on itself is the whole point. Chaining at the end would fling the reader to the next slide while they were browsing sources.

Phase 5 · Slide 3 — מה נעשה מאז in progress

DIA-384. The stages the item reached, one page each. The first screen with two scroll axes at once, and the pattern slide 4 inherits in Phase 6.

One gesture, two jobs, and no code between them

The stack is a single scroller with nested scroll-snap: each stage page is a snap point, scroll-snap-stop: always. Scrolling on at the bottom of a stage takes the reader to the next; scrolling back at the top takes them to the previous. There is no listener, no threshold and no boundary detection anywhere in the slide.

Deliberate, and the one architectural instruction this phase carried. DIA-383 is open on the deck's own axis decision; a second gesture layer here would mean fixing the same thing twice and in two places. The boundary is a property of the scroller, so whatever DIA-383 eventually does, this slide inherits it.

A stage reopens where it was left

A stage the reader has not been to opens at its top. One they have already read reopens where they left it, for as long as the page is open. A deep link to #3-s2 is a first visit. A landing through jump mode is an ordinary entry and obeys the memory. A stage shorter than the frame has nothing to remember. Nothing survives a reload.

It is what makes scrolling back up bearable: the reader returns to the foot of the stage they just read rather than above everything in it. The position is recorded for whichever page is at the top, every frame the reader moves — and never while the stack is scrolling itself, because a driven scroll passes through positions nobody chose.

The locator is an indicator, not a control

Tapping a rung does nothing. Five slots; only the stages belonging to this slide are drawn and the rest keep their place as invisible spacers. One ring, on the stage being viewed.

Held slots are why a rung never moves between slide 3 and slide 4 — the same stage is in the same place on both, so the ladder reads as one object across a swipe. The current stage lost its own ring on 20 September: splitting reached from unreached made it redundant, since here it is always the last rung drawn.

The pill's row keeps its height whether or not the pill is in it

חזרה לשלב הנוכחי appears only when the reader is off the current stage, with its arrow pointing the way. The row it sits in is always there.

DIA-379 again: a chrome that changes height resizes the scroller, and resizing a scroll container mid-scroll makes the engine recompute its snap positions. That was an overshoot on the deck; here it would be a stage page jumping under a thumb.

The footer's arrows are the route for a reader who does not scroll

↑↓ שלבים in the middle of the docked footer. Two real buttons, drawn as the screens draw the glyphs.

Nothing on the deck may be gesture-only. The footer is rendered once as docked chrome rather than per slide, so the control is given to the deck and talks to the stack through an event — which is also why slide 4 gets it in Phase 6 without a second implementation.

The URL's tail follows the page

Moving between stages rewrites the hash to #3-s2 and so on, under the same push-once rule as the slides. A refresh lands back on the stage that was showing.

The deck owns the URL and the stack owns which page it is showing. The stack publishes its page on the deck's own root and the deck reads it from there, so neither has to import the other to agree.

What to watch

ThingWhere it bites
The gesture layer on a slide that scrollsDIA-383. A casual scroll is taken as a swipe, and the swipe direction disagrees with the gate. Slides 3 and 4 inherit the same scroller in Phases 5 and 6, so two faults are about to be three.
No affordance at the cutSlide 2's text runs past the bottom of the frame with no fade and no shadow. §5 asks for none, so none was invented — but "there is more" and "this is broken" are one thumb apart.
The chip costs a lineNaming the source type in Hebrew makes the chip about 90px wide, so it usually wraps below its sentence rather than sitting at the end of it the way §5 draws.
Gestures cannot be judged from hereSynthetic touch in Chromium measures an axis decision correctly and says nothing about how a gesture feels. The reverted lock passed every assertion written for it. Anything in this document about a gesture's feel was decided with a thumb, and the next one will be too.
