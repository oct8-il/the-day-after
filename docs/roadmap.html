What changes now

The hard part of leaving the prototype phase is not technical. It is deciding that the prototype is done, and then treating it as a source of truth rather than a thing to keep polishing.

Three things come out of the prototype, and nothing else counts as "the design":

  • A stylesheet. The 35 tokens and every rule in its <style> block. Copied, not translated.
  • A set of behaviours. Its render functions (renderItem, openParent, drawMap, publicThread, the intro sequences) are the component list, one to one.
  • A screenshot baseline. Four views, two themes, two widths, captured once from the frozen prototype. Every ported view is compared against it.

From today, work runs on three parallel tracks. Build moves the prototype into a real codebase. Data replaces illustrative content with real sourced claims — this is the long pole, and it starts this week, not after the build. Launch covers legal review, outreach and social. The date is fixed, so each week ends with a gate and, if necessary, a cut from the ordered list at the bottom of this document.

Launch-day product

A read-only ledger with real data. Sign-in and voting come after launch; on 7 October the opinion chapter shows the stage question and says the vote opens soon.

Ships on 7 October

  • Failure matrix home (הכשלים) with headline strip and first-visit opening sequence
  • Gap view (הפער)
  • Item pages: chapters 1–2 fully live; chapter 3 shows the current-stage question with ההצבעה תיפתח בקרוב
  • Evidence map inside items that have placed claims
  • Methodology / about page in prose, corrections page
  • Real data: 9 parents, ≥ 20 incidents, every incident with at least one linked stage-1 claim
  • Share images made by the site itself: a link-preview card per item, plus Instagram post and story formats behind a share button (low priority within the MVP — see Social layer)
  • Hebrew, RTL, mobile-first, light and dark

Deliberately after launch

  • Google/Apple sign-in and Israeli phone OTP
  • Per-stage votes, thresholds, frozen chapters, results
  • Free-text answers, AI clustering, representative quotes
  • AI-drafted stage summaries with footnotes (launch uses editor-written summaries for the deepest items only)
  • Archive snapshots for claim URLs (the field exists in the schema; filled after launch)
  • Daily source scan and review queue
  • Timeline, browse-by-place, עמותה registration

The reason to leave voting out is not that it is hard to build — it is that it cannot be half built. A vote button that exists implies verification, thresholds, a privacy story and a moderation story, all of which have to be right on day one or they become the story. A ledger with no vote is complete; a ledger with a broken vote is not.

Social layer

The site produces its own share images. Not a screenshot someone takes of it — an image the site renders, in its own typography and colours, that looks right in an Instagram feed and links back to the item.

What it is

  • Three formats per item and per parent: link card 1200 × 630 (the Open Graph image every WhatsApp, X and Facebook preview shows), post 1080 × 1350 and story 1080 × 1920.
  • Content is what the tile already carries: icon, name, the stage ladder with the current stage lit, one sentence the site can produce automatically — 1,096 ימים אחרי, עדיין בשלב 2 — and the site mark. Institutions only, no photographs, no individuals: the editorial rule applies to images exactly as to text.
  • A share button (שיתוף) on item and parent pages opens a sheet with the three formats; on a phone it hands the image to the system share sheet (Web Share API with files), which is how it reaches Instagram. On desktop it downloads.

How it is built without a second design

Rule 1 applies here too. The share image is a page — /share/[id]/[format] — styled by the same globals.css, rendered at build time by the same pinned Playwright browser the fidelity gate uses, and saved as PNGs under public/share/. The site stays static, Hebrew and RTL render exactly as they do in the browser, and every image is regenerated whenever the data changes. The obvious alternative, Next.js's built-in image generation (satori), is rejected: its right-to-left text handling is unreliable and it would mean a second stylesheet.

Priority

In the MVP, but scheduled last: the link-card format comes with the share route in W3 because every shared URL needs it; the post and story formats and the share sheet are W4 work and sit second on the cut list. If they are cut, the route and the CSS stay and they ship the week after launch.

Keeping the prototype's UI

Prototypes lose their quality in production for four predictable reasons. Each has a rule that prevents it.

Fidelity is lost at the seams: tokens get re-created instead of copied ("close enough" colours, spacing rounded to a framework's scale); markup gets re-invented to fit a component library (a library card is not your card); the parts that were never written down — hover, focus, empty and loading states, first-visit motion — are never ported because they were not in "the spec"; and real data breaks layouts that were designed on ideal data. None of these is a skill problem. They are a process problem, and the process below is the fix.

Rule 1

The prototype's stylesheet becomes the app's global stylesheet, verbatim, on day one.

No Tailwind, no CSS-in-JS, no design-token "translation". Tokens keep their names (--s1…--s6, --bub-pro, --t-press), classes keep their names. The framework is plumbing under the design, never a second opinion on it.

Rule 2

Components are the prototype's render functions moved, not redesigned.

One function becomes one component and must emit the same DOM. renderItem → ItemPage, openParent → ParentPanel, publicThread → PublicThread. The port test is a DOM diff, not an opinion.

Rule 3

A screenshot baseline is captured before the port starts and every view must match it.

Playwright renders the frozen prototype: home, gap, item (i13), about × dark, light × 390 px, 1280 px — 16 images committed to the repo. The same script renders the app; a pixel diff above a small threshold fails the check. This is the fidelity gate, and it runs in CI.

Rule 4

The prototype is frozen at a git tag the day the port begins. Design changes after that happen in the app.

Two living copies diverge within days and one of them becomes the lie. After prototype-v0.4-final, docs/prototype.html is a reference and a baseline, not a place to work. The urge to keep tinkering with it is the single biggest schedule risk in this document.

Rule 5

Port with real data, not the prototype's data, and fix what breaks in the one stylesheet.

A 90-character incident title, an incident with one claim, an incident with forty, a parent with two incidents in a cell — the layouts that survive these are the layouts that ship. Fixes go into globals.css, so the design stays in one file the way it does today.

Rule 6

Motion and states are features with tickets, ported last but never dropped silently.

The opening sequence, the chapter gating on scroll, the ladder climb, the docked header and chapter rail, hover and focus states, prefers-reduced-motion. They are listed in the cut order so a decision to skip them is explicit and recorded, not something that just didn't happen.

What this looks like on day one

  1. Tag the prototype. Commit the uncommitted stage-colour change first or discard it; either is fine, but the tag must be deliberate.
  2. Run one script that extracts the prototype's JS constants (PARENTS, INCIDENTS, PLACES, STAGES, TYPES, Q) to JSON. That JSON is the seed of the data repo and its shape is the first draft of the schema.
  3. Scaffold Next.js (static export), paste the <style> block into globals.css, add the Google Fonts link, deploy the empty shell to the real domain.
  4. Capture the 16 baseline screenshots and commit them.

After that day the design is safe: it lives in a stylesheet, a data file and sixteen pictures, and nothing about the build can lose it without a red check.

Architecture at launch

As small as the concept doc allows. No database, no server, no auth. Everything is a file in a public repo and a static site built from it.

Repo, CI and deploy

The repo is currently local only, on master, with no remote. Putting it on GitHub with three branches and a pipeline is week-one work, and it is what makes "data as code" mean something.

GitHub

  • One repository, the-next-day, created and pushed in W1. Three long-lived branches, each one an environment: dev, staging, prod. dev is the default branch.
  • Private until the content freeze on 4 October, public on launch day. The methodology promises a public ledger, and the corrections page is rendered from the repo's history — but a public repo full of illustrative data before launch can be screenshotted out of context.
  • Code goes straight to dev in small commits — one person, no ceremony. Data goes through pull requests, even solo, targeting dev like everything else. A PR is the review queue: CI validates the claim, the diff is the audit trail, and the commit landing on prod is the "published" event the corrections page reads. After launch the daily scan opens these PRs by itself and nothing about the process changes.
  • Promotion is always the whole branch, never a cherry-pick: dev → staging when a gate passes, staging → prod when the integrated check is green. Because nothing is skipped, the three branches never diverge and each promotion is a fast-forward — three commands, no conflicts. A correction after launch is a PR to dev followed by two promotions; if promoting everything on dev is not acceptable at that moment, a hotfix branch off prod is merged to prod and immediately back into staging and dev.
  • Tags mark the moments the document refers to: prototype-v0.4-final when the port begins, v1.0.0 on prod on 7 October.

One honesty about this model for a static site: "real data" is not a property of an environment, it is a property of the branch, because the data ships inside the build. So staging is real-data by discipline — it is the branch that only ever receives whole promotions from dev, and the integrated checks (link liveness, share images, Lighthouse) are what make it worth a separate environment. The moment real data lands on dev, dev is "real data" too; the difference between the two is which checks have run and whether a human has looked. Before launch, prod is an empty shell behind the domain, and staging is where the site actually lives for five weeks.

GitHub Actions

One workflow, three tiers: the first three jobs run on every push and pull request to any branch; the integrated jobs run on staging; the release jobs run on prod.

  1. Validate — every file in data/ against the zod schema and the rules: no URL, date or type → no claim; stage 5 needs a non-implementer source; place only on claims; no names of individuals in any text field (a denylist check, not a guarantee).
  2. Build — Next.js static export. A broken build never reaches a preview.
  3. Fidelity — Playwright renders the four views at 390 and 1280, light and dark, in a pinned browser, and diffs against the sixteen baselines. Threshold per image; a failing diff is uploaded as an artifact so the difference is visible without checking out the branch.
  4. Integrated, on staging — every claim URL is fetched and must answer; the same browser renders /share/… to PNGs and diffs them against the previous set; Lighthouse runs on the item page at mobile settings and fails below an agreed score.
  5. Release, on prod — build, commit the share PNGs back so the deploy is fully static, regenerate the corrections page from the data history, tag.

Vercel

Chosen over Cloudflare Pages because it runs Next.js with nothing to configure, deploys every branch on its own URL, and takes custom domains with automatic TLS in minutes. prod is the Production branch on the apex domain; staging and dev are branch deployments each assigned a subdomain, plus a preview URL for every pull request. An environment variable per branch tells the build which it is, so staging and dev render a small environment ribbon and a noindex tag and prod does not. The free Hobby tier covers a non-commercial civic site; password-protecting the non-production URLs is a paid feature, so before launch they are simply unlisted and unindexed, which is enough for the risk involved. No secrets exist at launch; when Supabase arrives its keys live in Vercel's per-environment settings, which map onto the same three branches. Because the output is a static folder, moving hosts later is an afternoon, not a project.

Reproducibility

One person is a bus factor of one. The mitigation is that everything is in the repo and npm ci && npm run build reproduces the whole site, images included, on any machine.

Cut order

If a weekly gate fails, cut from the top of this list, never from the bottom. Cutting is a decision, so it gets a line in the decision log.

  1. First-visit opening sequence and item intro animationShip the pages at rest; the sequences return in v1.1. Everything else about the design survives.
  2. Instagram post and story formats and the share sheetThe link card stays, because every shared URL needs one. The route and CSS stay too; the formats ship the week after launch.
  3. Gap viewIts data is already visible on the tiles; the view is a second way in.
  4. Evidence map inside itemsThe source list stays; the map is its visual aid.
  5. Editor summaries for the deepest itemsThe ledger alone is still complete and honest.
  6. Number of incidentsTwelve fully sourced incidents beat twenty-five with gaps. Quality of sourcing is never cut.

Never cut: a linked source on every claim; CI on every change; the methodology page; the item page's three-chapter structure; mobile quality; both themes.

Risks

HIGH

Data curation takes longer than the build.

Mitigation: it starts in week 1 in parallel, incident count is last on the cut list, and the inclusion rule is strict enough that "not enough incidents" is an honest state, not a failure.

HIGH

Continuing to refine the prototype instead of porting it.

Mitigation: Rule 4. The tag is the line. Design ideas after the tag go into an issue list for the app.

MED

Holidays remove working days from three of the five weeks.

Mitigation: the calendar above assumes they are lost; if they turn out to be free days, the gap view and the animations come back off the cut list.

MED

Legal review finds a problem late.

Mitigation: review happens on the live preview with real data in W4, and the sourcing rules are the defence — nothing on the site is not already published elsewhere.

MED

Launching into the anniversary news cycle, which begins on 3 October this year.

Mitigation: the quiet launch on the 5th to people who will care is the real launch; the 7th is the public date.

LOW

Three branches drift because something was cherry-picked or committed to staging directly.

Mitigation: branch protection on staging and prod allows merges only; the promotion is a two-line script in the repo so it is never done by hand.

LOW

Fidelity check is too strict and blocks on font rendering noise.

Mitigation: threshold per image, and the comparison runs in one pinned browser in CI, never on a laptop.

Open questions

  • Domain name — in progress, needed by end of W1 for the share cards and the legal review.
  • GitHub: under the personal account, or an organisation created now for the future עמותה. An org is free and transferring later is annoying; lean org.
  • Repo visibility before launch: private until the 4 October freeze is the current plan; say so if it should be public from day one.
  • What the share image is allowed to say: the stage sentence and counts only, or also the incident's one-line description. Lean stage sentence only — it is the site's voice, not a claim.
  • The name-denylist check in CI: worth building for launch, or an editorial checklist item until the daily scan exists.
